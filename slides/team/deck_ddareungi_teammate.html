Ddareungi is losing riders. In 2024 there were about 120,000 rides a day; in 2025, about 104,000. That is 14% fewer, and it is not one bad month: every single month of 2025 is below the same month of 2024. In October a newspaper reported that the city was considering cutting the fleet. The city denied it the same day, but it confirmed that the Seoul Institute is studying the right size of the system and how to run it more efficiently.

STA3115 Exploratory Data Analysis, Group 1

So where does the money go? According to Bike Magazine, 53% of the operating cost is labour for redistributing bikes. That is the truck you see on the street, picking bikes up at one station and dropping them at another. Trucks are needed because some stations run dry while others overflow. So our question is: where do bikes pile up, where do they run out, and why? If we can answer that from data, the trucks can go to the right place at the right time.

Next, weather. Each dot is one weekday: temperature across, total rides up. On dry days the points follow a smooth LOESS curve: rides rise with temperature and dip a little in the hottest weeks. On rainy days, with 1 millimetre or more, rides drop and the points scatter. The residual plot makes it clear. On dry days the residuals are small and even, with a standard deviation of about 13,000 rides. On rainy days the spread is three times larger and grows with temperature. That means rain’s effect depends on things outside our data, like when and how hard it rains. So we set rainy days aside and keep dry weekdays.

STEP 2. WHAT WE MEASURE

378 dry weekdays, 2,843 stations, every trip

Net outflow

Rentals minus returns at a station. Positive: the station loses bikes. Negative: it gains them.

Measured for the whole day and for the 7–9 am commute, and divided by the number of docks so big and small stations compare.

Data

Every station-to-station trip in 2024–2025 (Seoul Open Data, 5-minute records), hourly weather from KMA, station elevation from 1:5,000 contours, subway stations and land-use zones.

Dry weekday = not a weekend or holiday, and less than 1 mm of rain. Ten days whose rentals and returns did not match were dropped.

STA3115 Exploratory Data Analysis, Group 1

That leaves 378 dry weekdays in 2024 and 2025, and 2,843 stations. Our measure is net outflow: rentals minus returns. If it is positive, the station loses bikes over that period; if negative, it gains them. We compute it for the whole day and for the morning commute, and we divide by the number of docks, so a big station and a small one can be compared. A station with a daily net outflow of plus 40% loses bikes equal to 40% of its docks every day, and a truck has to bring them back.

STA3115 Exploratory Data Analysis, Group 1

To see how, we turned every trip into a flow field. For each pair of places we take trips from A to B minus trips from B to A, and add them up into one arrow per square kilometre, drawn on top of the terrain. Darker ground is higher. Now colour each arrow by whether it points downhill or uphill. Where the ground slopes, 69% of the net flow runs downhill and only 7% uphill. Zoom into Seodaemun, Jongno and Seongbuk, where there are hills and many commuters: among the 120 biggest net flows between neighbourhoods, 58% run downhill and just 1% uphill. People ride down, and walk or take the bus back up. The bikes stay at the bottom.

So what does this mean for the trucks? Should a truck rush to every station that is emptied in the morning? Not necessarily. If residents ride back home in the evening, the station refills itself by night, and no truck is needed. We call that the self-balancing hypothesis. To test it, we ask every station two questions: is it emptied in the morning, and is it still short at the end of the day?

To see why, compare the two groups hour by hour. G2, in orange: bikes leave in the morning, and the evening commute brings them back; by night the station is full again. G1, in red: the same morning drain, but in the evening only a trickle comes back, about 1.3 bikes per station against 5.7 for G2. So what separates them? Elevation. G1 stations sit at a median of 28 metres, G2 at 19. Distance to the subway is almost the same, 500 against 470 metres. People ride down to the station in the morning, and in the evening they do not ride back up the hill.

CONCLUSION

Send the trucks up the hills, and skip the stations that refill themselves

Bikes roll downhill in the morning and are not ridden back up: net flow runs downhill 69% of the time where the ground slopes.

An empty station at 9 am is not always a problem. 703 stations (G2) refill themselves by night.

The 893 hill-top stations that never refill (G1) are where the trucks are needed, and the 533 low-lying stations that pile up (G3) are where to collect the bikes.

To sum up. Bikes roll downhill in the morning and are not ridden back up. Because of that, an empty station at 9 am is not always a problem: 703 stations get their bikes back by night without any help. The stations that really need a truck are the 893 G1 stations on the hills, and the natural place to collect bikes for them is the 533 G3 stations on low ground that pile up all day. Matching those two groups, instead of reacting to every empty station in the morning, is where the redistribution cost can come down.

LIMITS

What this analysis cannot tell us

1

Trip records hide trucks: we see where bikes go, not where trucks already move them.

2

A station that runs empty records no rentals, so demand on the hills is likely higher than we see.

3

Weekdays without rain only: weekends and rainy days need their own study.

4

We describe patterns; we do not estimate the cost a new truck plan would save.

STA3115 Exploratory Data Analysis, Group 1

Some limits. First, trip records do not show the trucks, so we see where riders move bikes, not where trucks already move them back. Second, when a station is empty, nobody can rent, so demand on the hills is probably higher than our numbers show. Third, we only looked at dry weekdays. And fourth, this is exploratory: we describe the pattern, we do not estimate how much a new truck plan would save.
