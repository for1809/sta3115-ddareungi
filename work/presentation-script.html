1 · Exploring Bicycle Imbalances About 0:19 · elapsed 0:00–0:19

Hello everyone. We’re Group 1, and today we’ll talk about Ddareungi, Seoul’s public bike service. Many of you have probably used it around campus or elsewhere in Seoul. We explored how bicycles move between stations and the imbalances that arise.

2 · How Ddareungi works About 0:36 · elapsed 0:19–0:55

[Point briefly to the photos, then follow Rent → Ride → Return.]

First, a quick introduction for anyone who hasn’t used it. You scan a bicycle’s QR code in the app, ride to your destination, and lock it at a station to return it.

The important part is that you don’t have to return it where you started. That makes the service convenient, but it also means bicycles keep moving between stations. Some places gain bikes, while others lose them.

3 · Why look at redistribution? About 0:48 · elapsed 0:55–1:42

[Point to the deficit first, then the 53% figure.]

Our starting point was the cost of running the service. The operating deficit increased from 9.4 billion won in 2022 to 17.6 billion won in 2025—almost double in three years.

That led us to look at redistribution: moving bicycles from stations with a surplus to those with shortages. According to Bike Magazine, staff time spent redistributing bicycles accounts for 53% of operating cost.

So we wanted to understand the imbalances behind that work. Our aim was to provide useful information for assessing redistribution needs, rather than calculate cost savings or design truck routes.

4 · Our question About 0:25 · elapsed 1:42–2:08

[Pause briefly so the audience can read the question.]

With this in mind, we asked: what station characteristics are associated with persistent bicycle imbalances, and why do some stations experience shortages?

We wanted to connect two things: how bicycles move, and whether a bicycle is actually available when someone wants to rent one.

5 · Data and scope About 0:35 · elapsed 2:08–2:43

We used station-to-station trip records from 2024 and 2025. We focused on non-holiday weekdays with less than one millimetre of rainfall, leaving 378 days.

Our main trip analysis covers 2,843 stations with records on at least 50 selected days. We also used hourly inventory records from 2025 to check availability, together with weather, elevation and subway-location data.

The exact number of stations varies slightly between comparisons, depending on which records we can match.

6 · Losing bikes versus running out About 1:20 · elapsed 2:43–4:03

[Reveal definitions and statistics as you explain them. Point right of zero, then above the dashed 20% line.]

Before looking for explanations, we needed to separate losing bikes from running out.

Net outflow is rentals minus returns. A positive value means a station loses bicycles through user trips. Empty share is the proportion of its morning stock readings—at 7, 8 and 9 am—with zero bikes available.

For example, five rentals and three returns give a net outflow of two. That station could still have plenty of bicycles left.

Each dot here is a station. Moving right means greater morning net outflow; moving up means more frequent empty readings.

About 63% of stations are on the right: they lose bikes on average in the morning. But only 24% of those are above the dashed line, empty in at least one in five readings.

Stations below that line can still run out. The point is that net loss alone doesn’t tell us how frequently it happens. We need both measures.

7 · Why commuting weekdays? About 0:44 · elapsed 4:03–4:47

[Reveal weekdays, then weekends, then the study-window emphasis.]

We first considered time of day and weather. On weekdays, these two sharp peaks appear at 8 am and 6 pm, following the commuting hours.

Weekends and holidays look different: there’s one broad afternoon peak instead.

Weather also affects how much people ride. That’s why we focused on dry weekdays, especially the morning commute, to compare movements under more consistent conditions. We kept the detailed temperature findings in the appendix.

Within that scope, we next looked at station characteristics.

8 · Elevation and net outflow About 1:15 · elapsed 4:47–6:01

[Begin with dots. Reveal below 20 m, the middle band, 40 m+, then the LOESS curve.]

This is our main elevation plot. Each dot is a station. Elevation is on the horizontal axis, and average daily net outflow is on the vertical axis.

Remember, above zero means bicycles leave on balance; below zero means bicycles arrive on balance.

Below 20 metres, stations gain about 1.5 bikes a day on average. At 40 metres and above, they lose about 4.3. Around 90% of stations in that higher group have positive daily net outflow.

The red smoothing curve makes the pattern easier to see: it rises with elevation.

There is still considerable variation between stations. We’re describing an overall tendency, rather than saying every station at the same elevation behaves identically.

So higher stations tend to lose bicycles through user trips. Whether that translates into shortages is the next part of the story.

9 · The geographical pattern About 0:40 · elapsed 6:01–6:42

[Terrain first → reveal red net-loss stations → reveal blue net-gain stations. Let the audience look.]

Here is that pattern geographically. The shaded areas are ground at least 50 metres high.

Now we add stations losing more than one bicycle a day, in red. Many appear around the hills. The blue stations gain more than one bicycle a day and are common along lower-lying areas.

The pattern isn’t universal, but the map helps us see where the relationship in the previous plot occurs.

10 · Distance to the subway About 0:50 · elapsed 6:42–7:32

[Point to the net-outflow panel, then the empty-share panel.]

Next, we looked at subway distance. The stations are divided into four distance groups.

On the left, the direction of morning movement changes. Stations closest to the subway gain about 1.5 bicycles, while those farthest away lose about 2.9. That is consistent with people riding toward the subway.

But look at the right-hand panel. Empty share increases from 9.6% to 12.2%—a less pronounced contrast than we see across elevation groups.

So subway distance helps describe where bicycles move, while the shortage pattern looks different. This is another reason to keep net outflow and empty share separate.

11 · Comparing station characteristics About 1:08 · elapsed 7:32–8:40

[Indicate the three rows, then the correlation and adjusted odds-ratio columns. Do not read every coefficient or p-value.]

We then compared three characteristics together: elevation, local relief within 300 metres, and subway distance. Local relief describes how much the surrounding ground varies in height.

The first column shows each characteristic’s rank correlation with empty share. Elevation has the strongest correlation, at 0.50.

The logistic model considers all three together. Its outcome is whether a station is empty in at least 20% of morning readings.

Elevation still has the clearest association: every additional 10 metres is associated with about 2.6 times the odds of being in that frequently empty group, after accounting for the other two characteristics.

That is an odds comparison, not a claim that the probability of a shortage becomes 2.6 times as large.

The takeaway is the elevation association, rather than treating the model as a redistribution forecast.

12 · Which way do bicycles move? About 1:16 · elapsed 8:40–9:56

[Show citywide arrows → reveal direction colors → reveal Yonsei–Sinchon inset. Keep Whole day selected for the 69% figure.]

To explore a possible explanation, we looked at direction. For each pair of places, we offset trips in opposite directions, then summarize the remaining net movement.

For example, ten trips one way and six back leave a net flow of four in the first direction.

On the left, each arrow represents a one-kilometre cell. Red means downhill; blue means uphill. On sloping ground, about 69% of the net-flow magnitude points downhill, compared with 7% uphill. These percentages describe net flow, not all individual trips.

On the right, we zoom into the familiar Yonsei–Sinchon area. Red arrows point toward lower ground, while blue arrows point toward higher ground. Many of the strongest connections here also run downhill.

This is consistent with bicycles moving downhill without an equivalent uphill return flow. It doesn’t tell us how individual riders travel home.

13 · Starting inventory and shortages About 1:11 · elapsed 9:56–11:07

[Compare starting bars first, then stock changes, then 5% versus 28%.]

But movement alone doesn’t tell us whether a station runs out. The number of bicycles available at the start also matters.

Here we compare flat and hill stations, both far from the subway. Flat stations start with about 19 bikes and still have 15 at 9 am. Hill stations start with about six and end with about five.

Here, flat means below 20 metres, and hill means 40 metres or higher. Both use the same bar scale.

Now look at empty share: 5% on flat ground, compared with 28% on the hills.

A larger stock decline does not necessarily mean more shortages. Flat stations retain a much larger buffer; hill stations are empty more frequently despite a smaller average decline.

These are group averages, and observed stock changes can also include movements other than user trips.

14 · Introducing recovery About 0:21 · elapsed 11:07–11:28

[Use the original slide as a short transition. Explain net-flow recovery; do not repeat its “needs no truck” claim.]

Starting inventory helps us understand shortages. A separate question is whether the bicycles lost in the morning come back later.

Later net returns might offset the morning loss—or they might not. To explore that, we classified stations using morning and full-day net outflow.

15 · Understanding G1–G4 About 1:19 · elapsed 11:28–12:47

[Introduce column/row questions → reveal groups → reveal hourly plots → highlight the right-hand G1/G2 column.]

The columns ask whether a station loses bikes during the morning commute. The rows ask whether its net outflow over the full day is also positive. Both are based on averages across selected days.

Focus on the right-hand column. Both G1 and G2 lose bikes in the morning.

For G1, rentals still exceed returns over the full day. For G2, later net returns offset the morning loss.

G3 has no net loss in either window. G4 has no morning net loss, but does have full-day net loss.

The small lines show hourly net outflow, not inventory. We’ll focus on G1 and G2: 1,005 and 766 stations. These groups describe recovery through user flow, not whether a station needs a truck.

A line below zero means returns exceed rentals during that hour. It does not mean the station has a negative number of bicycles.

16 · Different evening recovery About 1:12 · elapsed 12:47–13:59

[Reveal G2 → add G1 → reveal boxplots. Show Elevation first; briefly switch to Distance to subway if time permits.]

So, what differentiates these two groups?

On the left, positive values mean bikes leave, while negative values mean bikes arrive on balance.

Both groups lose bikes in the morning, but G2 receives more net returns in the evening. Between 5 and 8 pm, G2 gains about 5.3 bikes per station, compared with only 1.2 for G1.

On the right, the boxplots show station characteristics. The line inside each box is the median. G1’s median elevation is 29 metres, compared with 19 for G2. Their subway distances are similar.

Each box covers the middle half of its group. There’s overlap, so elevation doesn’t perfectly separate the stations.

So weaker evening recovery is associated with higher elevation here. That supports our interpretation, although it doesn’t establish that elevation causes the difference.

17 · Insights for redistribution About 0:40 · elapsed 13:59–14:39

There are three points we’d like you to take away.

First, elevation is associated with persistent bicycle imbalances.

Second, net movement tends downhill, and G1 stations show weaker evening recovery.

Third, hill stations start with fewer bicycles and experience shortages more frequently.

Together, these patterns can help the city assess redistribution needs. That means considering both which stations need attention and whether bicycles are available before the morning commute. We’re offering evidence to inform that assessment, rather than prescribing a specific truck schedule.

18 · Key limitations About 0:39 · elapsed 14:39–15:17

Before we finish, three limitations matter most.

First, unmeasured factors—such as land use, trip purposes or cycling infrastructure—may explain part of the associations. We cannot establish causation.

Second, unmet demand is unobserved. Someone who arrives and finds no bike leaves no rental record, so recorded usage doesn’t capture everyone who wanted to ride.

Third, hourly inventory readings can miss short shortages between snapshots. Our empty share describes those recorded observations, rather than the exact proportion of time a station is empty.
